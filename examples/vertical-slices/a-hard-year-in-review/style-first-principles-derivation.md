I want to know whether the way I handled this year was a mistake worth correcting or a cost worth accepting. I will not start from a verdict. I will start from what I can state and grant, and see where it leads.

**Premises**

- **P1 (an observation).** The Meridian initiative closed in March, after eighteen months, when the primary funder withdrew. The proposal was technically sound and the coalition held. The withdrawal itself was outside my control.
- **P2 (an observation).** Eleven people gave volunteer labor to Meridian. I thanked them inadequately, and I have not corrected that.
- **P3 (an observation).** Celeste and I had been close for six years. The drift began in April and became undeniable by June. We have not spoken since August.
- **P4 (an observation).** In April, Theo sent me a message. I read it and did not answer it.
- **P5 (a premise to grant or refuse).** An outcome I could not control does not, by itself, make my choices along the way wrong.
- **P6 (a premise to grant or refuse).** A choice is wrong if I made it to avoid a cost that I would have been able to bear.
- **P7 (a premise to grant or refuse).** Each unanswered obligation gets harder to meet the longer it waits, because the silence becomes a second thing to explain.

**Derivation**

Step 1. From P1 and P5, the funder's withdrawal is not a mistake of mine. The closure is therefore not the thing to audit.

Step 2. Step 1 leaves a different question open: what did I choose while the closure was approaching? I did not collect that evidence at the time, so I can only test the choices I remember. I kept the coalition aligned around optimism when some members needed a harder truth earlier. I asked what that choice avoided. It avoided a difficult conversation with eleven people, and a difficult conversation is a cost I could have borne. From P6, that choice was wrong, even though Step 1 says the outcome was not.

Step 3. From P2 and P7, the thanks I owe the eleven people is harder to give now than it was in March. The delay has added a cost to the debt without reducing the debt.

Step 4. From P3 and P7, the same holds for Celeste. I called the drift independence. Under P6, I was avoiding the cost of saying what I was going through, and I could have borne that cost. The silence since August is now a second thing to explain.

Step 5. From P4 and P7, Theo's unanswered message follows the same pattern as Steps 3 and 4. It is the smallest instance, and it is the one I can still close in a single sitting.

Step 6. Steps 2, 3, 4, and 5 share one cause. In each case I chose the quieter option because I judged the cost of the direct option to be too high. By P6, I judged wrongly each time.

**The load-bearing premise**

P6 carries the chain. If P6 is false, so that some costs of directness really were too high for me to bear, then Steps 2 through 6 fail, and what remains is a year in which I did what I could. I do not think that is true, but a reader who rejects P6 should stop here, and my conclusion should not bind them. P5 matters second: if it is false, the closure itself becomes mine to audit, and Step 1 fails.

**Consequence**

If the premises hold, then the year does not show that deep investment is a mistake. It shows that I paid for avoiding the direct conversation, and that the payment grew each month I waited. The next actions follow from Steps 3, 4, and 5 and from P7, and they are ordered by how fast the cost is growing: answer Theo, write the account the eleven people are owed, and decide what I want to say to Celeste before another month adds to the silence.
