# Why two anchor days: a derivation

Most arguments about return-to-office begin from a position and defend it. This one begins from six premises. Grant or refuse each one before reading further, because everything below rests on them and on nothing else.

## Premises

1. **P1 (inputs, from our own last two hiring cycles).** We compete for talent in markets where we have no office, and a five-day in-office rule would have removed those candidates from the pool.
2. **P2 (observation, reported by senior leaders).** Decisions among people who have never shared a room sometimes stall in threads that would once have resolved in a corridor conversation.
3. **P3 (observation, reported by contributors).** Many current employees relocated or rearranged childcare because remote flexibility was described to them as sustainable.
4. **P4 (definition, offered as a premise).** An unplanned conversation needs two people to be in the same place at the same time. Nobody can schedule it, but somebody can arrange the overlap.
5. **P5 (premise).** A rule that is stated in advance can be evaluated by a candidate or employee. A rule that is left vague cannot, and it tends to be read as the strictest version of itself.
6. **P6 (premise).** Focused individual work is done better in stretches that nobody interrupts.

## The chain

**Step 1, from P4.** Unplanned conversations cannot be mandated, but the overlap they need can be. So the question to answer is how many days of overlap to require, not how to require conversation.

**Step 2, from Step 1 and P2.** If stalled decisions come from missing overlap, then some required overlap addresses them. Zero required days leaves the stall in place, and the second premise says the stall is real.

**Step 3, from Step 1, P1, and P3.** Every required day of overlap costs something. By P1, the cost falls on candidates outside commuting range. By P3, it falls on current employees who built their lives around flexibility. Five required days charges both groups the maximum. The cost rises with each day, and the benefit in Step 2 comes from having any overlap at all. Whether a fifth day adds much over a second day is not something these premises establish, so this derivation does not claim it.

**Step 4, from Step 2 and Step 3.** The required number of days is therefore above zero and below five. Premises P1 through P4 alone do not pick a number inside that range. They only exclude the two ends.

**Step 5, from P6.** Focused work wants stretches that nobody interrupts. Every required day takes one such stretch away from people in focus-heavy roles. This pushes the number down from five without pushing it to zero, because Step 2 still holds.

**Step 6, from P5 and Step 4.** Whatever number we choose, it must be stated in advance and fixed. A vague range such as "a few days a week" would, by P5, be read as five. So the choice should be a small, named set of days. Naming the days also guarantees that the overlap in Step 1 actually falls on the same days for everyone, which a free choice would not.

**Step 7, from Step 4, Step 5, and Step 6.** A small number, fixed and named, is two: it is the smallest number above one that still leaves most of the week unrequired. One required day would give a single shared day that any absence or holiday erases entirely. Two days leave a second chance for the same people to overlap in the same week. I mark this as the weakest step in the chain, and I come back to it below.

## Consequence

If P1 through P6 are granted, the policy that follows is two fixed anchor days per week, Tuesday and Thursday, on which everyone who can physically reach an office is expected to be in one, and three days on which each person chooses their own location. The same chain shows that the policy will not satisfy everyone. P3 says some employees will bear a real cost, and Step 3 says that cost cannot be removed, only kept small and stated early. Priya Ahluwalia's working group, which has carried this position into the leadership briefing, can therefore say plainly what each premise buys and what it charges.

## The load-bearing premise, and what follows if it fails

The premise that carries the most weight is **P2**. Every step toward requiring any overlap at all rests on it. If P2 is false, so that stalled decisions turn out to come from unclear ownership and not from distance, then Step 2 fails and the chain ends at zero required days. In that case the correct policy is full flexibility, and the money and goodwill spent on anchor days is wasted. A reader who rejects P2 should reject the conclusion, and should say so.

The weakest inference is Step 7, where two is chosen over one. A reader who grants every premise but prefers one anchor day has found a gap in the derivation, not a flaw in the premises, because the premises exclude zero and five and say nothing sharper. I would rather name that gap here than let two stand as if it had been proven.
