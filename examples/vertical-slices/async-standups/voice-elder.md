I have run a daily standup, in one form or another, for about fifteen years, and for most of those years I believed that the ten minutes of standing in a circle were the thing that held a team together. I no longer believe that. I am not sure I was ever right, and I will say plainly that I was wrong about it for longer than I like to admit.

Here is what I can tell you about this team. There are eleven of us now, spread across four timezones, and the meeting sits at 9am Pacific, which is 9:30pm for the three engineers in India. Over the first quarter they came to an average of 3.2 of the five standups each week, and the engineers in the United States came to 4.6. For a long time I read that gap as a difference in commitment. It took me far too long to see that it was simply the hour, and that people who stay up until half past nine at night to say three sentences are not the ones who care least.

The meeting itself runs about fourteen minutes, and when I counted, about four of those minutes changed what anyone did next. I have seen that ratio in other teams in other years, and I have seen it defended each time, usually by me.

What I remember most is Priya working out a particular 401 error out loud, in the meeting, and five hours later another engineer hitting the same error and spending more than an hour finding it again. Nobody had done anything wrong. The answer had simply been said into the air, and the air does not keep things.

So we are going to try it the other way: a post in #team-standup by 10am local time, three fields, and a 60-minute working session on Thursdays for the conversations that need a room. We will run it for 30 days, and if the team wants the old meeting back, it returns the following Monday.

I cannot tell you how this will go. I have watched teams move to written updates and find they had been missing each other less than they feared, and I have watched others quietly lose the habit of talking, and I could not have told you beforehand which team would be which. The Thursday session may be enough to keep the team close, or it may not, and I do not know. In a year this will most likely be a few sentences in the story the team tells about itself, and I would rather we tell it truthfully than tell it as a success.
