I have kept one day of rest each week for two weeks, after earlier attempts that each collapsed within a few weeks. Before I defend the practice to myself again, I want to derive whether it should exist at all, using only what I have observed in my own weeks. Here are the premises. Refuse any of them that you cannot grant.

**Premises**

- **P1.** I have a fixed amount of attention to spend in a week, and I spend it on decisions, analysis, and the small choices between them.
- **P2.** Attention is spent faster the longer I work without a full stop. I observed this by comparing weeks: late in an unbroken stretch, decisions take longer, I repeat analysis I should have done once, and my patience for hard problems narrows. This is an observation from my own weeks, offered as an input, not as a general fact about people.
- **P3.** Ambient monitoring (checking the inbox, scanning the queue, tracking what is incoming) draws on attention even when it produces no output. I observed that giving it up for a full day felt like setting down a rope, which suggests it was carrying weight.
- **P4.** A day without work output and without monitoring is a different kind of day from a lighter workday, because a lighter workday still contains the monitoring in P3.
- **P5.** On the week ending June 21, 2026, two problems I had circled for days cleared on Monday after a kept rest day. This is one data point, labelled as one.

**Derivation**

*Step 1, from P1 and P2.* If attention is fixed and is spent faster as a stretch lengthens, then the hours late in an unbroken stretch return less per hour than the hours early in it. A seventh day of work is therefore the least productive day of the seven, not an average one.

*Step 2, from Step 1.* If the seventh day is the least productive day, removing it costs less output than its hour count suggests. The loss in raw hours is real, and Step 1 says the loss in usable hours is smaller than it.

*Step 3, from P3 and P4.* A lighter seventh day keeps the monitoring, so it keeps the draw on attention that P3 describes. Only a day that removes both output and monitoring removes the whole draw. That is why the rule must be "not mostly," and why it names what I will not do rather than what I must do.

*Step 4, from Step 3 and P2.* If the stretch resets only when the draw stops entirely, then a full stop is what interrupts the accumulation in P2, and a partial stop does not. This also accounts for the micro-recovery I already had in place, which left the pattern unchanged: it shortened the stretch's work without ever stopping the monitoring.

*Step 5, from Step 4 and P5.* If the stop interrupts the accumulation, then the first day back should show restored attention on the problems that accumulation had stalled. P5 shows exactly that, once. One instance does not establish the effect, and I treat it as consistent with the chain rather than as proof of it.

*Step 6, from Steps 2 and 4.* The cost of the practice is one day of output that Step 2 says was cheap output. The return is a reset that Step 4 says only a full stop provides. The trade is favourable on the premises as stated.

**Conclusion**

It follows that one full day in seven, kept completely, is worth its cost. The practice is not a virtue I am adding to my week. It is a consequence of how my attention behaves, given what I have observed of it.

**What carries the weight**

The load-bearing premise is P2. Steps 1, 2, 4, and 6 all rest on it. If P2 were false, and attention did not degrade across an unbroken stretch, then the seventh day would be an ordinary day, Step 2 would fail, and the rest day would cost a full day of average output for no reset. The conclusion would reverse. P5 is the weakest premise on evidence, but nothing in the chain depends on it alone; Step 5 would simply lose its support.

If you reject the conclusion, I think you reject P2. Check your own weeks before you grant it.
