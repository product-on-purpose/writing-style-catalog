# Why the phone stays in the kitchen until 7:30am

I want to work out what my first hour after waking should contain, starting only from things I can check against my own mornings. Each premise below is something you can grant or refuse. Nothing is built on one until it has been stated.

## Premises

- **P1 (input, from my own log).** I wake at 6:30am on weekdays. Family obligations begin at 7:30am and the work day begins at 9am. The first hour is the only stretch before 9am that nobody else has a claim on.
- **P2 (input, from my own log).** When the phone is within reach at waking, I use it for about 45 minutes. That time goes to Slack, email, and news, and none of it is chosen in advance.
- **P3 (input, from my own history).** I have tried "just stop using my phone in the morning" three times in the past year. Each attempt lasted four to six days, and each one removed the behavior without putting anything in its place.
- **P4 (assumption).** What I do in the first hour sets how reactive or deliberate I am for the rest of the morning. I cannot prove this. It is the premise I most expect you to test.
- **P5 (assumption).** An action I have to resist each time costs more than an action I never face. Resisting is a decision, and I make worse decisions when I am tired.
- **P6 (definition, to be granted).** A "deliberate" hour is one in which every action was named before I started it.

## Derivation

1. From P1 and P2, the phone currently consumes 45 of the 60 minutes I control. Fifteen minutes remain, and I did not choose how to spend them either.
2. From P6 and step 1, the present hour contains no deliberate action that I can point to. The problem is therefore not a shortage of time. It is that the time has no named content.
3. From P3, removing the phone alone leaves 45 empty minutes. The earlier attempts failed in four to six days, so the gap evidently refills with the old behavior. Whatever I do must therefore fill the 45 minutes with named actions, and not only subtract the phone.
4. From step 3 and P1, the named actions must fit inside 60 minutes and must end before 7:30am, when other people's claims begin. If four actions are chosen, they must average well under 15 minutes each to leave buffer for dressing and coffee.
5. From P5, an action that depends on my resisting the phone will fail on the tired days, which are the days that matter. The phone should therefore be out of reach, and it should not merely be ignored. Placing it in another room removes the decision at the moment I am least able to make it.
6. From P4 and step 5, the first named action should be one that can be done without any screen, so that the first input of the day is one I chose. Water, daylight, movement, and writing on paper all meet this condition. The order among them does not follow from any premise above, and I am not claiming that it does.
7. From steps 4, 5, and 6, a fixed sequence of four short actions with the phone in the kitchen until 7:30am satisfies every constraint derived so far.

## Consequence

The routine I should adopt is water, then ten minutes of light, then fifteen minutes of movement, then ten minutes of paper planning, with the phone plugged in and face down in the kitchen until 7:30am. This totals 35 minutes of named content, which leaves 25 minutes of buffer inside the hour. It follows from the premises. I did not start from it.

## What this does not settle

Step 6 fixes the first action but not the order of the other three. If movement should come before light, nothing here says so, and I would reorder after the first month instead of defending the order I wrote.

## The load-bearing premise

P3 carries the most weight. If my three failed attempts failed for some other reason, such as a bad week or a missing alarm, then step 3 does not follow, and a bare phone ban might have worked. P4 is the next most exposed. If the first hour does not shape the rest of the morning, the routine is a pleasant habit and not a repair. A reader who rejects P5 can keep the phone on the nightstand and rely on willpower, and the derivation allows it, at the price of the tired days.
