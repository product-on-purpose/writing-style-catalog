Tidemark opens to the public on June 30. Before we ask you to try it, we want to show what a small team's feedback process has to do, built only from things you can check against your own week. Grant each premise or refuse it. If you refuse one, you will know exactly where you and we part ways.

**Premises**

1. **Feedback arrives in more than one place.** A small team hears from customers through a support thread, a sales call note, a chat message, and a ticket. Each of those lives in a different tool. This is an observation about how teams work, and you can count your own tools to test it.
2. **A priority is a comparison.** To say one request matters more than another, someone has to hold both in view at once.
3. **A person can only compare what they have read recently.** Someone reconstructing the picture by hand reads some of the items, and the ones they read last weigh most.
4. **Input from the full set is the fairer basis for a priority than input from a recent slice.** This is a judgment, and we label it as one.
5. **A roadmap is only useful to the people who can see it.** If a customer, a new hire, or a colleague has to ask one person what is coming, the roadmap exists in that person's head and nowhere else.
6. **Input, measured in our early-access cohort.** Twenty-two small teams ran the full loop, from scattered feedback to a shared roadmap, and none of them filed a support request. Every team named a separate consolidation spreadsheet as its main pain at intake. This is a measurement from our own cohort, offered as an input, and it is a small group.

**The derivation**

From premise 1, the signals for any single decision are spread across several tools, so no single tool holds the whole set.

From that step and premise 2, comparing two requests means someone must first gather them from separate tools. The comparison cannot happen where the feedback is stored.

From that step and premise 3, the person who gathers them by hand reads a subset, and recent items are over-represented in it. The resulting priority reflects what that person read last more than what customers said most.

From that step and premise 4, a priority built this way is a weaker basis than one built from the full set. A team that cannot afford to find out six months later that it served the wrong customers has a reason to want the full set in one place.

From premise 5, a priority that lives in one person's head fails a second test, separate from the first. Even a correct ranking does no work for the people who cannot see it.

Put the last two steps together. A team needs the full feedback set gathered without hand-reading, and it needs the resulting ranking visible to everyone who relies on it. That is a description of what the product must do, and it follows from the premises without assuming Tidemark exists.

Tidemark does those two things. It takes feedback from the tools a team already uses, ranks it across the full set, and produces a shareable roadmap link that stays current. Premise 6 is our evidence that the loop runs end to end for a small team without help, and it is the one step in this chain that rests on a number we collected ourselves.

**Conclusion**

If you grant premises 1 through 5, a small team that tracks feedback across several tools is paying for a hand-built comparison that is biased toward recent items and hidden from most of its readers. Trying a tool that gathers the full set and shares the ranking is the consequence, and the free plan means the cost of testing it is a short setup.

**The load-bearing premise**

Premise 3 carries the most weight. If a person reading by hand could keep the full set in view without recency bias, the second step would fail and the case for gathering automatically would shrink to a convenience. Premise 6 is the weakest link in evidence, because twenty-two teams is a small sample and we chose neither the teams nor the intake wording blindly. If you refuse premise 6, the conclusion still follows from premises 1 through 5, and the only thing lost is our claim that setup is painless.
