## For Howard, on his last day

I have been writing these notes for about twenty years now, and for most of that time I believed the best thing a person could do at the end of a long stretch of work was leave behind good documentation. Howard's last day is today, the twenty-seventh of June, and I have spent most of this month watching the team try to write down what he knows. I was wrong for those twenty years, or at least I was wrong about Howard, and I would rather say so plainly than let it sit.

Howard came to us in June of 2000 as an operations coordinator, and twenty-six years later he is leaving as one. He was offered other titles twice, and he declined both times, and I remember being privately puzzled by it, the way you are puzzled when someone turns down the thing you assumed everyone wanted. It took me until about the fifth or sixth year of watching him to see what he had chosen instead. He had chosen to be where the work was, and the work was always at the level of the phone call, the vendor who would only speak to him, the two questions he asked before he told you anything.

I have seen four people leave this team after a long time here. The first three left a runbook, and the runbook was good, and within about eighteen months nobody could say with confidence what half of it meant, because the meaning had gone out the door with the person. I am not telling you that will happen again. I am telling you that is what happened the first three times, and that this month's work on Howard's runbook with Dana and Marcus was, I think, the best attempt we have made, and that I do not know whether it will hold. We will find out in the autumn, probably on a night when something breaks and he is not in the room.

At the send-off on the twenty-fifth, Howard spoke for about four minutes, and said he was proud of the team and did not want to be the reason anyone felt stuck. I have thought about that sentence more than I expected to. Some of you who came to him in your first two years will feel stuck anyway, around August, when the reflex to knock on his door is still there and the door is not. That is what happened after the others left. It passed for most people. It did not pass for everyone, and I cannot tell you in advance which kind of person you will be.

What I can say is about scale. Right now this feels like the loss of the one person who knew where everything was. In ten years I think it will be a smaller thing and a larger one at the same moment: a few sentences you say to someone new, beginning "there was a man here who used to." Several of you are already carrying more of him than you know. Priya and Ben and the others who sat with him over the years kept no record of what he taught them until this month, when they were asked and wrote some of it down, and Howard never did, and that is rather the point of how it worked.

I do not have advice for you. I have thought about whether I should, and I find that what I have is a report, and the report is that this is hard, that it eases at an uneven pace, and that nobody I know found a way to shorten it. If you have a number, a workaround, or something he said to you that never made it into the runbook, Carolyn would like to hear it by the eleventh of July. That is a request and not a lesson.

Howard, the extension is still in the directory, and nobody has had the heart to remove it. Thank you for twenty-six years. I am not sure what comes next for any of us, and I am glad you were here for the part that has already happened.
