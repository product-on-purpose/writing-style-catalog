Dear Dana,

I owe you a thank-you, and I want to show you how I arrived at the size of it, so that you can check my reasoning instead of taking my word for it.

**What I am granting, and asking you to grant.**

1. Ten years ago I was not ready to lead the project you put me forward for. I told you so at the time, and you nominated me anyway. This is an observation, and you were in the room.
2. You had a team of your own, your own commitments, and your own reputation. Time spent on my project was time not spent on those.
3. If the project had failed, the question people asked would have been about your judgment in naming me, not about mine in accepting.
4. You stayed close through the first months and did not take over when taking over would have been faster.
5. Last month I put someone on my team forward for a project she did not feel ready for, and I did what you did. This is also an observation, and it is the only reason I am writing now.

**What follows.**

From premises one and four: the work I did was mine. A project I led while someone else quietly made the hard calls would not have taught me what leading costs. Because you left the calls with me, I learned the cost at the time it was real.

From premises two and three: the help was not free to you. It was paid from your time and, in the worst case, from your standing. A gift that costs the giver something is larger than the same gift that costs nothing, so the thank-you has to be larger than a thank-you for advice.

From premise five and the step above: I could do the same thing last month only because I had already seen it done. I did not read it anywhere or hear it described. I watched it happen to me. So what you gave me was a pattern as well as a project, and a pattern is used again each time I act on it. The benefit is still arriving, and it now reaches someone you have never met.

From the three steps together: what I owe is more than gratitude for one project that went well. I owe you for the project, for what it cost you, and for every person I will now treat the same way.

**Where this could be wrong.**

The load-bearing premise is the fourth one. If you had quietly steered more than I remember, then the work was less fully mine, and the step about learning the cost at the time it was real weakens. I do not think so, because the moments I remember best are the ones where you asked a sharper question and then waited. But that is my memory, and you are the one who can correct it.

If premise five were false, and I had not repeated the pattern, the conclusion would shrink to a thank-you for one good project. It would still be real, but it would not be why I am writing this month.

**So.**

Thank you. The debt is larger than I priced it when I first sat down to write, and I wanted you to see the working that got me to the size of it.

With gratitude,
