## Marketer

The marketer voice is organized around a claim the reader has not yet accepted. It opens where the reader already is, inside a problem they recognize and described in language they would use themselves, and only then names the thing being sold. That order is not a stylistic preference. A reader who has not yet agreed that they have the problem has no reason to care what solves it.

What separates this voice from ordinary enthusiasm is that it is specific and falsifiable. It says "cuts a three-hour migration to eleven minutes," not "dramatically accelerates your workflow." The specificity is what makes the claim worth reading, and what makes it possible to be wrong. A marketer voice that cannot be wrong has stopped saying anything.

It addresses someone outside the organization, which is the constraint that shapes everything else. The executive announces a decision to people who must act on it. The product thinker aligns colleagues on which problem is worth solving. The marketer has no authority over the reader at all, and so everything it gets, it gets by being useful or interesting first.

### Language patterns

- Opens on the reader's problem, in the reader's vocabulary, before naming the product
- Concrete and falsifiable claims: "eleven minutes" rather than "dramatically faster"
- Second person throughout; the subject of most sentences is "you", not "we"
- Benefit stated before mechanism, with the mechanism kept only where it is the proof
- Short sentences and short paragraphs, built for a reader who is scanning
- Names the specific alternative the reader is living with today, not a strawman
- One call to action, stated plainly, rather than several competing invitations

### When to use

Use for landing pages and hero copy, product launches written for customers rather than for the team, ad copy under real character limits, and outbound email to someone with no prior relationship to the writer. Reach for this voice whenever the reader is deciding whether to buy, sign up, or try something.

### When not to use

Avoid for internal decision records, where persuasion is the wrong job entirely; for technical documentation, where the reader has already bought and now needs to succeed; for anything addressed to people the writer has authority over; and for bad news, incidents, or apologies, where selling reads as evasion. Avoid it also for regulated claims about health, finance, or safety, which must be hedged to remain accurate.

### Pairs well with

`confident`, `problem-solution`, `landing-page`

### Often confused with

**executive**: Both are brief, both lead with a claim, and both are comfortable being unhedged. The difference is standing. The executive is announcing a decision to people inside the organization who will act on it, and can assume the reader's attention as a matter of role. The marketer is addressing someone outside it who owes them nothing and may leave at any sentence. That is why the executive's subject is usually "we" and the marketer's is usually "you".

**product-thinker**: Both start from a problem rather than a solution, which is what makes them confusable. The product thinker is establishing agreement among colleagues about which problem is worth solving, and the audience is internal and collaborative. The marketer is establishing that a specific reader has the problem already and can stop having it, and the audience is external and deciding. One is aligning a team; the other is converting a stranger.

**columnist**: Both write to persuade a reader who has not agreed yet, and both use a confident first-person claim. The columnist argues a position they personally hold and stands behind it as their own view; the piece succeeds if the reader thinks differently afterward. The marketer advocates for a product rather than a personal position, and the piece succeeds if the reader acts. A column that ends in a purchase has changed genre.
